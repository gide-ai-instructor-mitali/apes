UNIT 1

The Living World: Ecosystems

6–8% of AP Exam

1.1 — Introduction to Ecosystems

An ecosystem is the functional unit of ecology — a community of organisms interacting with one another and with the nonliving environment as a system. Ecosystems are open systems: they exchange both energy (one-way flow) and matter (cycled) with their surroundings.

All ecological components are classified as biotic (living: organisms at all scales) or abiotic (non-living: temperature, light, water chemistry, soil texture, wind, etc.). Abiotic factors set the physical stage; biotic factors determine who survives on it.

Ecological Hierarchy

From smallest to largest unit of ecological organization: Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biome → Biosphere. Each level has emergent properties not predictable from lower levels alone.

Habitat is the physical place where a species lives — its ecological "address." Niche is the totality of an organism's role: what it eats, when it's active, how it uses space, what it is eaten by — its ecological "profession." The fundamental niche is the full range a species could occupy without competition; the realized niche is what it actually occupies when constrained by competitors and predators.

The competitive exclusion principle (Gause's Law): two species with identical niches cannot coexist indefinitely — the superior competitor will eventually exclude the other. Real co-existence is enabled by resource partitioning: sympatric species evolve slight niche differences (temporal, spatial, dietary) that reduce direct competition sufficiently for stable coexistence.

📋
Exam High-Yield Habitat ≠ Niche. Habitat is WHERE; niche is HOW + WHAT + WHEN. Multiple species can share a habitat but cannot indefinitely share a niche.

1.3 — Aquatic Biomes

Aquatic biomes cover ~71% of Earth's surface. Key governing variables: salinity, light penetration (turbidity), dissolved oxygen (DO), temperature, and nutrient availability.

Estuaries are transition zones where rivers meet the sea. Variable salinity (brackish water) makes them highly productive due to nutrient loading from both terrestrial runoff and tidal circulation. They serve as nursery habitat for ~70% of commercially important fish species. Threats: coastal development, nutrient pollution, sea-level rise.

Coral reefs occupy <1% of ocean floor but support ~25% of all marine species. Built by scleractinian coral polyps and their endosymbiotic dinoflagellates (zooxanthellae), which provide >90% of the coral's energy via photosynthesis. Paradoxically, reefs flourish in warm, nutrient-poor, oligotrophic tropical water — their high productivity comes from extremely tight nutrient recycling.

Wetlands (marshes, swamps, bogs, fens) are among the most productive ecosystems per unit area. They sequester carbon, purify water, attenuate floods, and support enormous biodiversity. Despite this, >35% of global wetlands were lost between 1970–2015 (Ramsar Convention data).

1.4 — Primary Productivity

∑
Core Productivity Equations GPP = total photosynthetic rate (all energy fixed by autotrophs) NPP = GPP − Rₚ (Rₚ = producer respiration) NPP = energy available to primary consumers Units: g C/m²/yr OR kcal/m²/yr OR g dry biomass/m²/yr

Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) measures the total rate of photosynthesis — how much solar energy is converted to chemical energy in organic molecules. Net Primary Productivity (NPP) is what remains after autotrophs burn some of that energy in their own cellular respiration. NPP is the actual biomass available to heterotrophs (consumers + decomposers).

Primary limiting factors for NPP: light (depth, turbidity, cloud cover), temperature, water (terrestrial), nutrient availability (especially N and P). In most freshwater systems, phosphorus is limiting (Schindler Experimental Lakes Area). In most marine systems, nitrogen is often limiting at low latitudes, iron in high-latitude open oceans (Martin's iron hypothesis).

1.5 — Biogeochemical Cycles

Essential elements cycle through the biosphere via biogeochemical cycles, moving between living organisms (bio), geological reservoirs (geo), and the atmosphere/hydrosphere. The speed of cycling and the size of reservoirs govern nutrient availability. A key distinction: carbon and nitrogen have significant atmospheric reservoirs; phosphorus does not.

1.7 — Energy Flow & The 10% Rule

Energy flows unidirectionally through ecosystems — unlike matter, it cannot be recycled. This is a direct consequence of the Second Law of Thermodynamics: every energy conversion produces some entropy (heat waste). At each trophic transfer, organisms use energy for: metabolic maintenance, thermoregulation (endotherms), locomotion, reproduction, and excretion. Only the energy actually stored in new biomass (about 5–20%, with ~10% as a useful rule-of-thumb) is available to the next level.

∑
Trophic Efficiency Formula Ecological Efficiency = (Energy at level n+1) / (Energy at level n) × 100% ≈ 10% between trophic levels Example: Producers fix 50,000 kcal → Herbivores get 5,000 → Carnivores get 500 → Top carnivores get 50 kcal

This efficiency constraint explains why food chains rarely exceed 4–5 levels, why top predators require enormous territories, and why a vegetarian diet has a far smaller energy footprint than a meat-based diet. Ecologically, it also explains why large apex predators are the first to disappear from ecosystem fragments — insufficient energy base for viable populations.

🔬
Current Research: Trophic Downgrading A landmark 2011 Science paper (Estes et al.) coined the term "trophic downgrading" to describe the global loss of apex predators and mesopredators. The cascading effects — increased herbivore browsing, vegetation loss, altered fire regimes, river channel changes — are now documented on every continent and major ocean, representing one of the most pervasive anthropogenic impacts on Earth's ecosystems.

1.8 — Food Webs & Trophic Cascades

Real ecosystems are food webs, not food chains — most species eat and are eaten by multiple other species. Web complexity (number of links) generally correlates with ecosystem stability: more links mean more redundancy and alternative energy pathways if any single species is lost.

A keystone species exerts disproportionately large effects on community structure relative to its biomass. Removal triggers a trophic cascade — non-linear changes propagating through the food web. The term was coined by Robert Paine in 1969 based on his sea star (Pisaster) experiments in the Pacific intertidal.

The Yellowstone wolf reintroduction (1995) is the most studied trophic cascade: wolves → altered elk behavior and numbers → riparian vegetation recovery → stream geomorphology changes → beaver and songbird recovery. Critically, the "landscape of fear" effect (behavioral changes in prey avoiding risky habitat) proved as important as direct predation in driving the cascade — a major advance in modern predator-prey research.

The Living World: Biodiversity

6–8% of AP Exam

2.1 — Biodiversity: Three Levels of Analysis

Biodiversity is most precisely understood at three nested scales: genetic diversity (allelic variation within a species — the raw material for evolution and disease resistance), species diversity (richness × evenness), and ecosystem diversity (variety of habitat types and communities across a landscape).

Species diversity combines two components: richness (number of species present) and evenness (relative abundance distribution). A community of 10 species where all are equally abundant has higher diversity than one where 90% of individuals belong to a single species, even with the same richness. The Shannon diversity index (H') quantifies both components.

Genetic diversity is fundamentally important for conservation: small, inbred populations with low genetic diversity are vulnerable to pathogens (the Irish Potato Famine was enabled by near-zero genetic diversity in cultivated potato varieties), environmental change, and inbreeding depression. Minimum viable populations (MVPs) are typically set at ≥50 individuals short-term and ≥500 long-term to maintain genetic health.

2.2 — Ecosystem Services

The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) and subsequent work has systematized ecosystem services into four categories:

  • Provisioning services: Tangible goods — food, clean water, timber, fiber, medicinal compounds, genetic resources. ~25% of all pharmaceuticals derive from tropical forest plants.
  • Regulating services: Climate regulation (carbon sequestration), flood attenuation, water purification, erosion control, disease regulation, crop pollination (pollinators responsible for ~35% of global food production), air quality regulation.
  • Cultural services: Recreation, ecotourism, aesthetic value, spiritual significance, educational use, sense of place.
  • Supporting services: Nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production, water cycling — the foundational processes that underpin all other services.

Costanza et al. (1997, updated 2014) estimated global ecosystem services at $125–145 trillion/year — roughly twice global GDP. Despite this, most ecosystem services are unpriced by markets, creating market failures where their destruction appears economically rational in the short term.

2.3 — Island Biogeography Theory

MacArthur and Wilson's Theory of Island Biogeography (1967) predicts equilibrium species number on islands as the balance between immigration rate (species colonizing from the mainland) and extinction rate (species lost from the island). Two island properties govern these rates:

  • Island size: Larger islands have lower extinction rates (more habitat, larger populations, lower stochasticity risk) and higher immigration (larger target area). Result: more species.
  • Distance from mainland: Closer islands have higher immigration rates. Result: more species.
FIG 2.3 — ISLAND BIOGEOGRAPHY: EQUILIBRIUM MODEL

This theory applies directly to habitat fragments — forest patches, parks, and nature reserves isolated by human development function as ecological islands. Smaller, more isolated fragments have higher extinction rates and lower immigration rates, predicting lower biodiversity at equilibrium. This insight drives the SLOSS debate in conservation (Single Large Or Several Small reserves) and the design of wildlife corridors.

2.4 — Ecological Tolerance

Every species has a range of tolerance for each abiotic variable (Shelford's Law of Tolerance). The tolerance curve is approximately bell-shaped: an optimal range flanked by zones of physiological stress, then zones of intolerance at the extremes. Population density and fitness are highest near the optimum.

Liebig's Law of the Minimum: the factor most outside a species' tolerance range limits its distribution — even if all other factors are optimal. This is why adding the limiting nutrient (phosphorus to most lakes) produces dramatic responses, while adding non-limiting nutrients has little effect.

🔬
Climate Change & Tolerance Limits As climate change shifts abiotic conditions faster than species can adapt or migrate, specialists with narrow tolerance ranges face accelerating extinction pressure. Range shifts are documented globally: species moving poleward (~17 km/decade average) and to higher elevations (~11 m/decade), tracking their thermal envelopes — but many species cannot move fast enough, or suitable habitat doesn't exist at their new latitude/elevation.

2.5 — Natural Ecosystem Disruptions

The Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis (Connell, 1978) proposes that species diversity peaks at intermediate disturbance frequency and intensity: frequent, severe disturbance allows only pioneer (r-selected) species; very infrequent disturbance allows competitive dominants to exclude most species; intermediate disturbance maintains a mix of early- and late-successional species.

Disturbances create a heterogeneous "shifting mosaic" of habitat patches at different successional stages across a landscape, maximizing landscape-level biodiversity. This explains why forest managers use prescribed fire and why rivers meander — these disturbances maintain diverse habitats.

2.6 — Ecological Succession

Primary succession begins on lifeless substrate with no soil: glacial outwash plains, fresh lava flows, newly exposed bedrock. Pioneer species (crustose lichens, cyanobacteria) tolerate extreme physical conditions and begin soil formation through mechanical weathering and organic matter accumulation. From bare rock to climax forest may take thousands of years.

Secondary succession occurs after disturbance on substrate retaining soil, seed banks, and biological legacies. The trajectory is much faster — decades rather than millennia — because biological infrastructure is preserved. Classic sequence in abandoned northeastern US fields: Annual weeds (1–2 yr) → Perennial grasses/forbs (3–10 yr) → Shrubs (10–20 yr) → Early deciduous trees (20–50 yr) → Late successional (climax) forest (50–200+ yr).

Modern ecology has moved beyond the classical deterministic climax concept. Multiple stable states can exist, and the final community is often more variable and pathway-dependent than Clements' original theory suggested. Human-influenced successional pathways (arrested by fire suppression, invasive species, etc.) diverge substantially from "natural" trajectories.

Populations

3.1 — Generalist vs. Specialist Species

Generalists tolerate a wide range of conditions (broad fundamental niche), exploit many food sources, and thrive in disturbed or variable environments. They tend toward r-selected life histories. Examples: common raccoon, white-tailed deer, American crow, house mouse.

Specialists are highly adapted to narrow conditions, often achieving greater efficiency within their niche than generalists, but with no flexibility outside it. Examples: koala (Phascolarctos cinereus, only eucalyptus); giant panda (>95% bamboo diet); black-footed ferret (obligate prairie dog predator). Specialists are disproportionately represented among threatened and endangered species.

UNIT 4

Earth Systems & Resources

10–15% of AP Exam

4.1 — Plate Tectonics

Earth's lithosphere is divided into ~15 major tectonic plates moving at 2–15 cm/yr on the viscous asthenosphere. Three boundary types: Divergent (plates separate → seafloor spreading, mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys; e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift); Convergent (plates collide → subduction zones create trenches + volcanic arcs; continent-continent collision builds mountain ranges like Himalayas); Transform (plates slide laterally → earthquakes, no volcanism; e.g., San Andreas Fault). Hot spots are stationary mantle plumes beneath moving plates, creating volcanic island chains (Hawaiian Islands).

📋
Plate Tectonics Connections Convergent boundaries → subduction → magma → volcanoes AND porphyry copper/gold deposits (hydrothermal mineralization). Divergent boundaries → seafloor spreading → Mid-Ocean Ridges → black smoker hydrothermal vents. Transform boundaries → earthquakes ONLY (no volcanoes).

4.2 — Soil Formation & Erosion

Soil formation (pedogenesis) proceeds over millennia through weathering (physical: freeze-thaw, abrasion; chemical: hydrolysis, carbonation, oxidation; biological: root acids, organism burrowing). Mature soil develops distinct horizons: O (organic litter), A (topsoil — humus + minerals, maximum biological activity), E (eluviation/leaching zone), B (illuviation — accumulates leached materials), C (weathered parent material), R (bedrock).

FIG 4.2 — SOIL HORIZONS PROFILE

Erosion causes: tillage, overgrazing, deforestation, construction. Prevention: no-till farming, contour plowing, terracing, cover crops, riparian buffers, windbreaks. The SMCRA (Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act, 1977) requires reclamation of surface-mined land in the US.

4.3 — Earth's Atmosphere

Layers (ascending): Troposphere (0–12 km; weather, temperature decreases with altitude); Stratosphere (12–50 km; ozone layer, temperature increases with altitude due to UV absorption); Mesosphere (50–80 km; coldest layer, meteors burn here); Thermosphere (80–700 km; aurora, ISS orbit); Exosphere (outer boundary).

Key GHGs: CO₂ (most important by volume and longevity), CH₄ (80× CO₂ GWP at 20 yrs), N₂O (265× CO₂ GWP), H₂O vapor (most abundant but short-lived, acts as feedback), CFCs (ozone destroyers + high GWP). GHGs absorb outgoing longwave (IR) radiation and re-emit it in all directions, warming the troposphere.

📋
Greenhouse Effect vs. Ozone Depletion Two SEPARATE issues. Greenhouse effect: GHGs in TROPOSPHERE trap IR radiation → warming. Ozone depletion: CFCs destroy O₃ in STRATOSPHERE → more UV reaches surface. CFCs contribute to BOTH issues. Never conflate them on the exam.

4.5 — Geography & Climate

Rain shadow effect: Moist air forced upward over mountains cools adiabatically, condensing and precipitating on the windward side. The now-dry air descends the leeward side, warming. Result: wet windward forests, dry leeward rainshadow deserts (Sierra Nevada / Great Basin; Andes / Atacama).

Ocean currents: Thermohaline circulation ("global conveyor belt") distributes heat worldwide. Warm surface currents (Gulf Stream) warm adjacent coasts; cold deep currents upwell nutrients. The AMOC is currently ~15% weaker than mid-20th century — a major concern for European climate and a potential tipping point under continued warming.

4.6 — El Niño & La Niña (ENSO)

ENSO is a quasi-periodic (every 3–7 years) oscillation in Pacific Ocean SST and atmospheric pressure. Normal: Trade winds push warm water west; cold upwelling off Peru. El Niño: Trade winds weaken → warm water spreads east → reduced upwelling → Peru fishery collapse; floods in Peru/Ecuador; drought in Australia/Indonesia. La Niña: Enhanced trade winds → stronger upwelling → wetter Australia; drier western South America; enhanced Atlantic hurricane season. Climate change is shifting ENSO patterns — more frequent and intense extreme events.

UNIT 5

Land & Water Use

10–15% of AP Exam

5.1 — Tragedy of the Commons

Garrett Hardin (1968): when a shared resource lacks enforceable governance, rational self-interest drives each user to maximize extraction, collectively destroying the resource. Key solutions: (1) privatization (align individual/collective interest through ownership); (2) government regulation (externally enforced limits); (3) community governance (Ostrom's polycentric approach — communities manage their own commons through legitimate rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions). Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics demonstrating that communities can successfully manage commons without privatization or government control, challenging Hardin's binary framing.

5.2 — The Green Revolution

1940s–1970s intensification package: HYV seeds (Norman Borlaug's dwarf wheat and semi-dwarf rice), synthetic N fertilizers, pesticides, irrigation, mechanization. Fed an additional ~1 billion people but created long-term sustainability challenges: aquifer depletion (Ogallala −0.5–3 m/yr), soil salinization, 90%+ loss of genetic diversity in major crops (monoculture risk), N₂O emissions (a powerful GHG), pesticide treadmill, and small farmer debt. The Second Green Revolution seeks to replicate yield gains with reduced environmental impact through precision agriculture, drought-tolerant GMO varieties, and soil health practices.

Waterlogging: Saturated soils exclude oxygen → anaerobic conditions → root death. Both are severe in arid and semi-arid regions with flood irrigation.

5.4 — Pest Control & IPM

Pesticide resistance is natural selection operating at human timescales — each application kills susceptibles, leaving resistant genotypes to reproduce. The "pesticide treadmill" requires ever-higher doses or new chemistries as resistance spreads. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines: monitoring (economic thresholds before treatment), cultural controls (crop rotation, planting timing, resistant varieties), biological controls (predators, parasitoids, pathogens), mechanical controls, and targeted chemical use as a last resort. IPM reduces total pesticide use 50–90% while maintaining effective pest control.

5.5 — Meat Production, CAFOs & Fisheries

Industrial animal agriculture (CAFOs) produces food efficiently by measure of land occupied, but generates: massive waste lagoons (manure runoff → eutrophication), CH₄ and N₂O emissions (~14.5% of global GHGs from livestock — FAO), antibiotic resistance (70%+ of all antibiotics used in livestock globally), and significant water consumption. Feed conversion ratios: beef ~7 kg grain/kg meat; chicken ~2 kg/kg — the higher the FCR, the greater the cascade of land, water, and energy use.

Overfishing: >34% of global fish stocks are overfished (FAO 2022); 60% are at maximum sustainable yield. Management tools: maximum sustainable yield (MSY) calculations, individual transferable quotas (ITQs), marine protected areas (MPAs), bycatch reduction devices (BRDs), ecosystem-based fisheries management (EBFM).

5.6 — Mining Impacts & Remediation

Surface mining (strip, open-pit) removes overburden, destroying habitat across vast areas. Acid mine drainage (AMD): pyrite (FeS₂) + O₂ + H₂O → sulfuric acid + dissolved iron — one of the most environmentally damaging and persistent forms of water pollution, persisting for centuries after mine closure. Treatment: passive (constructed wetlands, limestone channels) and active (lime neutralization, bioreactors). Tailings (mine waste) contain heavy metals (arsenic, mercury, lead, cadmium) — tailings dam failures (Brumadinho, Brazil 2019) cause catastrophic downstream contamination. SMCRA (1977) mandates reclamation of surface coal mines in the US, including restoring approximate original contour and establishing vegetation cover.

5.7 — Urbanization & Ecological Footprints

The urban heat island (UHI) effect arises from: low-albedo paving and rooftops absorbing solar radiation; reduced evapotranspiration (no vegetation); anthropogenic waste heat; urban canyon geometry reducing sky view factor. UHI magnitudes: 1–7°C warmer than surrounding rural areas, peaking on calm, clear nights. Mitigation: cool/green roofs, urban forests, permeable pavements, urban parks.

Impervious surfaces prevent precipitation infiltration → increased stormwater runoff velocity → channel erosion, flooding, and delivery of urban pollutants (oils, heavy metals, trash, pathogens) to receiving waterways. Low-Impact Development (LID) strategies: bioswales, rain gardens, permeable pavement, green roofs, rainwater harvesting.

The ecological footprint (Wackernagel & Rees, 1996) measures the biologically productive area needed to sustain a lifestyle. Global average ~2.7 ha/person; Earth's biocapacity ~1.7 ha/person → humanity is in 59% overshoot, drawing down natural capital rather than living on ecological income.

UNIT 6

Energy Resources & Consumption

10–15% of AP Exam

6.1 — Fossil Fuels & Nuclear Energy

Fossil fuels formed from ancient organic matter under heat and pressure over 300+ million years. They represent stored solar energy from past photosynthesis, making them effectively nonrenewable on human timescales. CO₂ emissions/energy: Coal (anthracite: 228 kg/MBtu) > Oil (161 kg) > Natural gas (117 kg). Coal also emits SO₂, mercury, NOx, and fine particulate matter.

Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) accesses previously unreachable shale gas/oil. Environmental concerns: methane leakage (GWP 80× CO₂ over 20 years); water consumption; induced seismicity; groundwater contamination risk. At >4–6% methane leakage rate, natural gas offers negligible or negative climate improvement over coal on 20-year timescales.

Nuclear fission: ~12 g CO₂e/kWh lifecycle emissions (comparable to wind); 92% capacity factor; high energy density (~1 kg uranium releases ~3 million× the energy of 1 kg coal). Challenges: radioactive waste storage (>10,000-year containment required), meltdown risk (Chernobyl, Fukushima), high capital costs ($90–200/MWh), long construction timelines, public acceptance.

6.4 — Energy Conservation & Efficiency

The most sustainable unit of energy is the one never consumed. The concept of negawatts (Amory Lovins, RMI) treats energy efficiency as a resource: a demand-side investment that eliminates the need for supply-side generation at lower cost and zero emissions. Major efficiency levers: building insulation and passive solar design; LED lighting (90% more efficient than incandescent); electric vehicle drivetrain efficiency (EV: ~77% efficient; ICE: ~20–25%); industrial process heat recovery; smart grid load management.

Policy tools: Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards; building energy codes (ASHRAE); ENERGY STAR labeling; carbon pricing (makes efficiency investments financially rational); utility decoupling (separates utility profit from energy sales).

7.2 — Photochemical Smog & Temperature Inversions

Photochemical smog: NOx + VOCs + UV light → ground-level O₃, PAN, secondary PM. Worst conditions: sunny days, high traffic, calm winds, warm temperatures, and critically — thermal inversions. In a temperature inversion, a warm air layer overlies cooler surface air, acting as a lid that prevents normal convective mixing and trapping pollutants near ground level. Notorious inversion episodes: London 1952 (4,000+ deaths in 4 days, ultimately 12,000+), Donora Pennsylvania 1948, Los Angeles summer smog seasons.

⚠️
Inversion Mechanism Normal: warm surface air rises, dispersing pollutants upward. Inversion: surface air cooled at night → cold dense air trapped below warm layer → no convection → pollutants accumulate. Valley cities and coastal cities in regions with offshore high-pressure systems are most vulnerable.

7.3 — Indoor Air Pollution

  • Radon (Rn-222): Odorless, colorless radioactive gas from ²³⁸U decay in granite, phosphate rock, and some soils. Seeps through foundations. ~21,000 lung cancer deaths/year in the US (2nd only to smoking). Mitigation: sub-slab depressurization systems.
  • Asbestos: Amphibole and chrysotile fiber types; causes mesothelioma, asbestosis. In pre-1980 buildings as insulation, floor tiles, and pipe wrap. Regulated under TSCA and AHERA.
  • Lead paint: Pre-1978; causes neurological damage, behavioral disorders, IQ reduction in children. Particularly dangerous when sanding or renovating old homes. Regulated under TSCA Lead Rule.
  • Biomass combustion: Wood/dung/crop residue burning in poorly ventilated homes — 3.8 million deaths/year globally (WHO) from IAP. Dominant health issue in low-income nations.

7.4 — Emission Reduction Technologies

Scrubbers (flue gas desulfurization): Limestone slurry or alkaline solution removes SO₂ from power plant exhaust by chemical neutralization. Removes 90–95% of SO₂. Electrostatic precipitators: Charge particles in exhaust, collect on charged plates; removes 99%+ of large PM. Baghouse filters: Fabric filters capture fine PM; highly effective for coal ash. Catalytic converters (automotive): Platinum/palladium/rhodium catalysts convert CO → CO₂, HC → CO₂ + H₂O, NOx → N₂. Reduces regulated automotive emissions 90%+. The Clean Air Act (1970, amended 1990) established NAAQS for 6 criteria pollutants (CO, SO₂, NOx, PM, O₃, Pb) and NESHAP for hazardous air pollutants. Since 1970: criteria pollutant emissions −78%, despite 275% GDP growth.

7.5 — Acid Deposition

Acid rain chemistry: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (sulfurous acid) → H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid in oxidized form); NOx + H₂O → HNO₃ (nitric acid). Normal rain pH ≈ 5.6 (CO₂ dissolution); acid rain pH <5.6, sometimes <4 in severe cases. Effects: leaches Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ from soils (nutrient loss, Al mobilization → toxic to fish); acidifies lakes (kills fish, amphibians — pH <5 lethal to most fish); corrodes buildings (calcite/limestone → CaSO₄). The US SO₂ cap-and-trade program (Clean Air Act Amendments, 1990) reduced emissions 67% by 2010, demonstrated market mechanisms can work for pollution control at lower-than-expected costs.

7.6 — Noise Pollution

Sound above 85 dB causes cumulative hearing damage; above 140 dB causes immediate damage. Marine noise from shipping (20–200 Hz, overlapping cetacean communication frequencies) masks whale communication, impairs navigation, and may cause strandings. NOAA and IMO are developing speed reduction programs and routing changes in whale habitats. Terrestrial noise disrupts bird song frequency and territory (urban birds sing at higher frequencies), masks predator/prey cues, and fragments wildlife movement.

Aquatic & Terrestrial Pollution

8.2 — Eutrophication & Biomagnification

FIG 8.2A — EUTROPHICATION PROCESS (Nutrient → Hypoxia)

Biomagnification occurs because lipophilic (fat-soluble) persistent pollutants (DDT, PCBs, methylmercury, dioxins) are not metabolized or excreted efficiently. Each consumer retains most of the toxin consumed → concentration multiplies at each trophic level. Classic example: DDT in Clear Lake, California (1950s): water: 0.02 ppb → plankton: 5 ppm → small fish: 40–300 ppm → western grebes: 1,600 ppm. Bioaccumulation = buildup within a single organism. Biomagnification = concentration increase up the food chain. Both occur simultaneously.

8.5 — Toxicology Fundamentals

Paracelsus (16th c.): "The dose makes the poison." All substances are toxic at sufficient dose; the question is the dose-response relationship. The LD50 (lethal dose for 50% of a test population) is the standard comparative toxicity measure — lower LD50 = more acutely toxic substance. Dose-response curves are typically sigmoidal: threshold → steep linear region → saturation.

Synergistic toxicity: Two chemicals together produce a greater combined effect than either alone (1+1>2). This is particularly important for real-world environmental exposures, where organisms encounter complex mixtures. Tobacco smoke + asbestos = synergistic lung cancer risk 50× greater than either alone. Most regulatory toxicology tests single compounds — a significant gap in risk assessment.

Acute vs. chronic exposure: Acute = short, high dose (CO poisoning, pesticide spill); Chronic = long-term, low dose (lead, mercury, endocrine disruptors). Many environmental health risks are chronic — the challenge is establishing causation across long latency periods. Endocrine disruptors (pesticides, plasticizers, pharmaceuticals) can produce effects at extremely low doses (parts per trillion), challenging traditional dose-response models.

UNIT 9

Global Change

15–20% of AP Exam — Highest Weight!

9.1 — Ozone Depletion & the Montreal Protocol

Stratospheric ozone (O₃ at 15–35 km altitude) absorbs 97–99% of harmful UV-B and UV-C radiation. CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons: Freon-12, Freon-11) used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, and foam-blowing agents are photolyzed in the stratosphere, releasing Cl atoms: CFCl₃ + UV → CFCl₂• + Cl•. Each Cl atom catalytically destroys ~100,000 O₃ molecules: Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂; ClO + O → Cl + O₂. The Antarctic "ozone hole" forms each September-October through heterogeneous reactions on polar stratospheric cloud (PSC) surfaces, enabled by extreme cold (<−78°C).

The Montreal Protocol (1987) mandated phaseout of CFCs. Universally ratified by 197 nations — the only environmental treaty with universal ratification. Ozone is recovering: the 2022 WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment projects stratospheric ozone returns to 1980 levels by ~2040 (tropics), ~2045 (northern midlatitudes), ~2066 (Antarctic). This represents perhaps the clearest success story in global environmental governance, demonstrating that international coordination can solve planetary-scale environmental problems.

9.2 — Global Climate Change

The enhanced greenhouse effect is driven by anthropogenic emissions: CO₂ (primarily fossil fuels + deforestation), CH₄ (natural gas, livestock, rice, landfills, permafrost thaw), N₂O (synthetic fertilizers, combustion), and F-gases (industrial). Global mean surface temperature has risen ~1.2°C above pre-industrial baseline (IPCC AR6, 2021).

Evidence for anthropogenic climate change (multiple independent lines):

  • Atmospheric CO₂ at 422 ppm — 50% above any Holocene value; isotopic signature (depleted ¹³C, zero ¹⁴C) confirms fossil fuel origin
  • Global mean surface temperature: warming trend in 5 independent datasets (NASA GISS, NOAA, HadCRUT, Berkeley Earth, JMA)
  • Ocean heat content increase (~90% of excess energy stored in oceans)
  • Sea level rise: +20 cm since 1900 (thermal expansion + ice melt); rate accelerating to ~3.7 mm/yr currently
  • Arctic sea ice extent declining ~13% per decade; Arctic warming 3–4× global average (Arctic amplification)
  • Ice cores: 800,000-year CO₂/temperature correlation (r = 0.93)

9.3 — Ocean Warming & Acidification

Oceans have absorbed ~90% of excess heat and ~30% of anthropogenic CO₂ since industrialization. Carbon chemistry: CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ ⇌ 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻. Increasing H⁺ → decreased pH (from ~8.2 pre-industrial to ~8.07 now; 26% increase in [H⁺]) → decreased CO₃²⁻ → calcifying organisms (corals, pteropods, oysters, coccolithophores) struggle to form CaCO₃ shells and skeletons.

The Great Barrier Reef has experienced 5 consecutive mass bleaching events (2016–2024), with recurrence intervals now shorter than recovery times — creating a chronic stress regime. Coral bleaching occurs when SST exceeds the thermal tolerance threshold by ≥1°C for extended periods, causing expulsion of zooxanthellae symbiotic algae. If temperatures don't return to normal within 6–8 weeks, corals die.

🔬
Ocean Deoxygenation A less-publicized but equally serious ocean stressor: global ocean O₂ has decreased ~2%, with oxygen minimum zones (OMZs) expanding ~7 million km² since 1960 (Keeling & Garcia, 2002; Oschlies et al., 2018). Mechanisms: warmer water holds less O₂ (Henry's Law); increased stratification reduces O₂ mixing to depth; coastal eutrophication creates dead zones. Mesopelagic fish (100–1000 m) are being compressed into narrower habitable depth ranges.

9.4 — Invasive Species

Invasive species are non-native organisms that cause ecological or economic harm in their new environment. They succeed because: no natural predators, competitors, or diseases in new range; r-selected life histories enable rapid population growth; often exploit altered/disturbed habitats that suit their generalist traits. IUCN lists invasive species as the 2nd largest driver of documented extinctions globally.

Notable examples: Zebra/quagga mussels (ballast water, Great Lakes — outcompete natives, filter water, alter food webs); Burmese pythons (pet trade, Everglades — 87–99% declines in multiple mammal species); kudzu (soil stabilization introduction, southeastern US — "the vine that ate the South"); cane toad (deliberately introduced for beetle control, Australia — toxic to native predators); emerald ash borer (accidentally introduced, North America — killed billions of ash trees).

9.5 — Biodiversity Loss & Conservation

The Holocene/Anthropocene extinction — the 6th mass extinction — proceeds at 100–1,000× background rates. Current extinction rate: ~24 known species per day (IPBES 2019). HIPPO framework for drivers: Habitat destruction (30%, #1 globally), Invasive species (11%), Pollution (14%), Population growth (human, indirect driver), Overexploitation (23%), + Climate change (14%, rapidly growing importance).

Conservation frameworks: Endangered Species Act (1973) — protects listed species and "critical habitat" in the US; CITES — regulates international trade in ~38,000 species; 30×30 goal (Global Biodiversity Framework, 2022) — protect 30% of land and ocean by 2030; wildlife corridors — maintain gene flow between fragmented habitats; marine protected areas (MPAs) — currently ~8% of the ocean, target 30%; rewilding — restoring ecosystems through keystone species reintroduction.

🔬
Biodiversity–Disease Connection (Dilution Effect) Emerging research (Ostfeld & Keesing) shows that high host diversity can reduce disease transmission in some vector-borne diseases. In fragmented forests, low diversity favors competent reservoir species (e.g., white-footed mice for Lyme disease), increasing tick infection rates and human risk. If confirmed broadly, biodiversity conservation may function as a direct public health intervention — a powerful new argument for ecosystem protection.
