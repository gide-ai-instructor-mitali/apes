Background
The urban heat island (UHI) effect causes city temperatures to be significantly warmer than surrounding rural areas, primarily due to impervious surfaces (asphalt, concrete), waste heat from vehicles and buildings, and reduced vegetation. Research shows that urban tree canopy can reduce surface temperatures through shading and evapotranspiration — the process by which trees release water vapour, cooling the surrounding air. Studies have also documented that low-income and minority neighbourhoods within US cities have, on average, 33% less tree canopy cover than high-income neighbourhoods (American Forests, 2021). Scientists working with three mid-sized US cities are investigating whether increasing urban tree canopy cover reduces maximum afternoon surface temperatures in residential neighbourhoods and whether this effect differs between low-income and high-income neighbourhoods.
Figure 1. Relationship between tree canopy cover (%) and maximum afternoon surface temperature (°C) in 60 urban residential census tracts across three cities
Each point = one census tract. Lines = linear regression fit for each income category. n = 30 tracts per income category, 3 cities pooled.
(a) Scientists propose that increasing urban tree canopy cover reduces maximum afternoon surface temperatures through two mechanisms: shading and evapotranspiration. Describe one of these two mechanisms and explain how it reduces surface temperature. (2 points)
(b) Scientists plan to design a controlled experiment using 20 identical vacant lots in a single city, randomly assigning 10 lots to receive tree planting (treatment) and 10 lots to serve as controls. Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable in this experiment. Describe what the control group will demonstrate in this experiment. (2 points)
(c) Using Figure 1, describe the relationship between tree canopy cover and maximum surface temperature. Identify one confounding variable, other than tree canopy cover, that could explain differences in surface temperature between low-income and high-income neighbourhoods. (2 points)
(d) Explain one specific human health consequence of the urban heat island effect that would disproportionately affect low-income residents. (1 point)
(e) Describe one specific long-term consequence of increased urban tree canopy cover on the urban water cycle, beyond temperature reduction. Explain the mechanism by which tree canopy produces this effect. (2 points)
(f) Propose one specific policy mechanism a city government could use to increase tree canopy cover specifically in low-income neighbourhoods, and describe how this policy would address the environmental justice concern documented in Figure 1. (1 point)
Model Answer — Question 1 (Investigation Design) 10 points available
Part (a) — Mechanism for temperature reduction 2 pts
Option A — Evapotranspiration (1 pt for naming + 1 pt for mechanism):
Trees absorb liquid water from the soil through their roots and release it as water vapour (H₂O gas) through pores in their leaves (stomata) — this combined process is called evapotranspiration. The phase change from liquid water to vapour requires a large input of latent heat energy (approximately 2,270 J per gram of water evaporated). This latent heat is drawn from the surrounding air and surfaces rather than from raising air temperature — the process is thermodynamically equivalent to the cooling effect of sweating. Trees in urban areas release hundreds of litres of water vapour per day, cooling the surrounding air and reducing maximum afternoon surface temperatures in the process.

Option B — Shading (1 pt for naming + 1 pt for mechanism):
Tree canopies intercept incoming solar radiation (shortwave energy) before it reaches impervious surfaces (asphalt, concrete, rooftops) below. Without shade, these surfaces absorb solar radiation and convert it to longwave (thermal/infrared) radiation re-emitted back into the surrounding air, dramatically raising local temperatures. Tree canopies absorb and reflect incoming solar radiation, preventing it from reaching and heating surfaces below. The resulting shaded surfaces remain significantly cooler than unshaded surfaces — reducing the total thermal energy stored and re-emitted into the urban air mass.
Must NAME one mechanism AND explain the physical/biological mechanism by which it reduces temperature. "Trees provide shade and that cools things down" earns no points — must explain WHY shade reduces temperature (less solar energy absorbed by surfaces below) or WHY evapotranspiration cools (latent heat of vaporisation removes heat from air).

Part (b) — IV, DV, and control group purpose 2 pts
Independent variable (IV): Presence or absence of tree planting (planted trees vs no trees planted). OR: Number of trees planted per lot / tree canopy cover (%).

Dependent variable (DV): Maximum afternoon surface temperature (°C) measured on each lot.

Control group purpose (1 pt): The 10 unplanted lots serve as the control group — they establish the baseline maximum afternoon surface temperature in identical lots without tree cover. Comparing temperatures in planted lots to the control lots allows scientists to determine how much of any temperature difference is specifically due to tree planting, rather than other factors. Without the control group, scientists could not attribute temperature changes to trees alone — temperatures might change for other reasons (weather, season, traffic patterns) unrelated to the treatment.
IV = what the scientists CHANGE or MANIPULATE (tree planting). DV = what they MEASURE (temperature). Control group purpose = establish baseline to compare; isolate the effect of the treatment. "The control shows what happens without trees" earns partial credit; "establishes baseline temperature to determine if any temperature change is due specifically to tree planting" earns full credit.

Part (d) — Health consequence disproportionately affecting low-income residents 1 pt
Heat-related illness and mortality (heat stroke, hyperthermia): During heat waves, extremely high surface and air temperatures in low-canopy urban neighbourhoods can cause heat exhaustion, heat stroke (core body temperature >40°C), organ failure, and death. Low-income residents are disproportionately affected because they are more likely to: (1) live in poorly insulated, poorly ventilated housing without air conditioning (air conditioning is costly — low-income households often cannot afford installation or operating costs); (2) work in outdoor or non-air-conditioned occupations (construction, agriculture, landscaping); (3) lack access to cooling centres or transport to reach them. The combination of greater UHI exposure (less canopy) and less adaptive capacity makes heat waves a life-threatening environmental justice issue.
Must specifically connect the UHI effect (elevated temperatures) to a human health outcome AND explain WHY it disproportionately affects low-income residents. "Heat is bad for people" earns no points. "Heat stroke/death from extreme temperatures in residents without air conditioning who cannot afford it" earns the point.
Part (e) — Urban water cycle consequence + mechanism 2 pts
Consequence (1 pt): Reduced stormwater runoff volume / increased groundwater recharge: Increased urban tree canopy significantly reduces the volume of stormwater that runs off into streets and storm drains during rain events.

Mechanism (1 pt): Tree canopies intercept rainfall — leaves, branches, and bark capture water droplets that would otherwise hit impervious surfaces and run off. Some intercepted water evaporates directly from leaf surfaces (interception loss). Additionally, tree root systems break up compacted urban soil, increasing infiltration rates — allowing more rainfall to percolate downward into the soil and groundwater aquifer rather than running off over sealed surfaces. Roots also take up water, which reduces soil saturation and increases the soil's capacity to absorb the next rainfall event. Together, interception and infiltration can reduce runoff volume by 10–40% depending on canopy density, reducing flood peak flows, combined sewer overflow events, and downstream streambank erosion.
Must state a SPECIFIC water cycle consequence (reduced runoff, increased infiltration, reduced flooding, increased groundwater recharge) — not just "trees help the water cycle." Then explain the mechanism: interception of rainfall by canopy + increased soil infiltration through root channels.
Part (f) — Policy for environmental justice + mechanism 1 pt
Policy: Targeted urban tree canopy equity program — A city could pass legislation or allocate funding specifically to plant trees exclusively in neighbourhoods with the lowest existing canopy cover (below a threshold, e.g., <15%), which research shows are disproportionately low-income and minority communities. The city could partner with community organisations to engage residents in species selection and planting locations, and commit to maintenance funding for newly planted trees. This directly addresses the environmental justice concern documented in Figure 1 — that low-income neighbourhoods have lower canopy cover and higher surface temperatures — by actively directing resources to the most heat-burdened and tree-deficient communities rather than allowing private tree planting to accumulate in wealthier areas. The Inflation Reduction Act (2022) designated Urban and Community Forestry funding that states can use for exactly this purpose.
Policy must be SPECIFIC (not just "plant more trees") and must explicitly describe how it addresses the environmental justice concern (targeting low-income areas with less canopy). Acceptable policies: targeted grants for tree planting in low-income areas; inclusion of urban forestry requirements in planning permits for development in low-income areas; public-private partnerships for greening disadvantaged neighbourhoods.

Background
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a group of over 12,000 synthetic chemicals that have been used since the 1950s in non-stick cookware, waterproof clothing, food packaging, firefighting foam (AFFF), and industrial processes. PFAS are extremely chemically stable — the carbon-fluorine (C-F) bond is one of the strongest in organic chemistry — making them highly persistent in both the environment and the human body, earning them the nickname "forever chemicals." In April 2024, the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) established the first-ever maximum contaminant level (MCL) for PFOA and PFOS in drinking water at 4 parts per trillion (ppt). Scientists have found elevated PFAS concentrations in groundwater and surface water near military bases, airports, and industrial sites across the United States. Model Answer — Question 2 (Environmental Problem and Solution) 10 points available
Part (a) — Point vs nonpoint source identification + justification 1 pt
Classification: Point source pollution.
Justification: The PFAS contamination originates from a single, identifiable, discrete location — the Lakeview Military Installation, where AFFF firefighting foam was used for training on specific designated areas of the base. The source is geographically identifiable and legally attributable to a specific facility (the military base), making it a point source. The data in Table 1 confirms point source characteristics — PFAS concentrations are highest at the well closest to the base (W-1: 88 ppt PFOA, 142 ppt PFOS) and decrease progressively with distance in a clear gradient pattern, consistent with contamination emanating from a single fixed source.
Point source = single identifiable, fixed location with discrete discharge. Nonpoint source = diffuse, multiple, no single entry point. The military base is an identifiable fixed facility → point source. The gradient pattern in Table 1 (highest near the base, decreasing with distance) is supporting evidence. Must JUSTIFY — not just say "point source."
Part (b) — PFAS pathway to groundwater 2 pts
Pathway (2 steps = 2 points):
Step 1 — Surface application and runoff: During firefighting training exercises from 1972–2018, AFFF containing PFAS was sprayed across training areas on the base. PFAS-containing foam and water pooled on the surface, contaminating soil and surface water at the training sites. Because PFAS have very low affinity for soil particles (they do not readily adsorb to soil organic matter the way many other pollutants do), they remain highly mobile in water.

Step 2 — Leaching/infiltration through the vadose zone to the aquifer: Rainfall and irrigation water percolated downward through the unsaturated (vadose) zone of the soil, carrying dissolved PFAS molecules with it. Because PFAS do not significantly adsorb to soil particles and are extremely chemically stable (the C-F bond is not broken by soil bacteria or environmental conditions), they migrated continuously downward through the soil profile until reaching the water table. Once in the saturated zone, dissolved PFAS became part of the groundwater flow system and were transported by groundwater movement toward nearby wells, with concentrations decreasing as the plume dispersed and diluted with distance from the source.
Two steps = 2 points: (1) AFFF applied to surface → PFAS in soil/water at surface; (2) PFAS leach through soil to groundwater (emphasise PFAS mobility = low soil adsorption + chemical persistence = not degraded by bacteria). Distance-related dilution/dispersion explains the gradient in Table 1.
Part (c) — Relationship + identification + calculation 2 pts
Relationship (1 pt): As distance from the military base increases, PFAS concentrations in drinking water wells decrease — an inverse (negative) relationship. PFOA concentrations fall from 88 ppt at 0.2 km to less than 1 ppt at 12.0 km. This pattern is consistent with contaminant dilution and dispersion as the PFAS plume moves through the groundwater system with distance from the source.

Identification and calculation (1 pt): Well W-1 (0.2 km from the base) has the highest PFOA concentration: 88 ppt.
Times above EPA MCL = 88 ppt ÷ 4 ppt = 22 times above the EPA MCL for PFOA.
Relationship must describe the direction (as distance increases, concentration decreases) and use data (cite actual values). Calculation: W-1 PFOA (88 ppt) ÷ EPA MCL (4 ppt) = 22×. Show the division. Both halves earn 1 point each.
Part (d) — Vulnerable population justification with Figure 2 data 2 pts
Pregnant women and infants are particularly vulnerable to PFAS contamination at W-1 (PFOA = 88 ppt, PFOS = 142 ppt — far exceeding the 4 ppt MCL) for two key reasons:

Health effect 1 — Low birth weight (1 pt): Figure 2 shows that chronic PFAS exposure is associated with a 20% increased relative risk of low birth weight. Fetuses are particularly susceptible because PFAS cross the placental barrier and accumulate in fetal tissue during critical developmental windows. Low birth weight is a serious health outcome associated with developmental delays, respiratory problems, and increased infant mortality. The extremely high PFAS concentrations at W-1 (88 ppt PFOA = 22× the MCL) would place pregnant women at substantial risk for this outcome.

Health effect 2 — Reduced vaccine efficacy / immune suppression (1 pt): Infants depend on vaccinations for protection against childhood diseases during a period when their immune systems are immature. Figure 2 shows a 15–30% increase in immune suppression and vaccine efficacy reduction with PFAS exposure. This means infants of mothers consuming water from W-1 may not develop adequate immune responses from standard childhood vaccines (measles, polio, diphtheria), leaving them vulnerable to preventable infectious diseases.
Must reference at least TWO specific health effects from Figure 2 AND explain WHY fetuses/infants are especially vulnerable (placental transfer; immature immune system; developmental windows). Simply listing Figure 2 effects without explaining the mechanism for infant/fetal vulnerability earns only 1 point.
Part (e) — PFAS removal technology + mechanism 2 pts
Technology: Granular Activated Carbon (GAC) Filtration
Mechanism (2 pts = technology + mechanism): Water from contaminated wells is passed through beds of granular activated carbon — charcoal that has been processed to create an enormous internal surface area (up to 1,500 m² per gram). PFAS molecules, despite being extremely stable in water, have hydrophobic (water-repelling) fluorinated carbon chains that are attracted to the hydrophobic surface of activated carbon through van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions. PFAS molecules adsorb (stick) to the carbon surface as water passes through, effectively removing them from the drinking water supply. GAC can reduce PFAS concentrations by 90% or more when properly sized and maintained. The carbon must be periodically replaced or thermally regenerated when saturated. GAC is EPA-approved for PFAS removal and is currently being required at many contaminated sites under the 2024 PFAS MCL.

Alternative: High-pressure membrane filtration (nanofiltration/reverse osmosis) — Forcing water at high pressure through semi-permeable membranes with pore sizes <1 nm physically excludes PFAS molecules (which are 1–14 nm in length), preventing them from passing through. Removes >95% of PFAS but produces a concentrated PFAS-containing brine waste stream requiring safe disposal.
Technology must be NAMED (GAC, reverse osmosis, nanofiltration, ion-exchange resins, foam fractionation). Mechanism must explain HOW it physically removes PFAS — adsorption to carbon surface (GAC), physical size exclusion (RO/NF), or ion exchange. "Filter the water" earns zero points. Must be specific about the mechanism.
Part (f) — 2024 regulation + limitation 1 pt
Regulation: EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulation for PFAS (April 2024) — established the first legally enforceable maximum contaminant level (MCL) for PFOA and PFOS in public drinking water systems at 4 ppt (parts per trillion).

Limitation: The 2024 PFAS MCL applies only to public water systems regulated under the Safe Drinking Water Act — it does NOT directly apply to private wells, which are the most common water source in rural areas near military bases where communities may rely on individually owned wells. Residents using private wells (like potentially those at W-1 through W-4 in Table 1) are not protected by this MCL — they must test their own wells and fund their own treatment systems without federal mandate. This creates a significant protection gap in communities near contaminated military installations where private well use is common.
Must NAME the 2024 EPA PFAS MCL regulation specifically. Limitation must be realistic and specific: (1) doesn't apply to private wells; (2) requires 5 years for compliance; (3) military installations may claim national security exemptions; (4) enforcement depends on state primacy; (5) doesn't address contaminated soil/sediment, only drinking water.

Background
Soil organic carbon (SOC) — the carbon stored in soil organic matter — represents the largest terrestrial carbon pool on Earth, containing approximately 1,500 Pg of carbon in the top metre alone. Conventional tillage agriculture accelerates SOC loss through oxidation and erosion, releasing CO₂ to the atmosphere. Regenerative agriculture practices (no-till farming, cover cropping, diverse rotations) can rebuild SOC over time. Model Answer — Question 3 (Soil Carbon / Regenerative Agriculture / Carbon Markets) 10 points
Part (a) — SOC loss mechanism + additional consequence 2 pts
SOC loss mechanism (1 pt): Conventional tillage physically breaks up soil aggregates that protect organic matter from microbial decomposition. When soil is ploughed, (1) previously protected organic matter is exposed to oxygen and soil bacteria, accelerating aerobic decomposition and releasing CO₂; (2) soil structure is disrupted, reducing the stable macro- and micro-aggregates that physically occlude organic matter from microbial enzymes; (3) tillage increases soil surface area exposed to sunlight and wind, accelerating drying and erosion which removes topsoil (the most SOC-rich layer). Over time, repeated tillage systematically depletes SOC, as shown by the decline from 2.10% to 1.75% in Table 1.

Additional consequence from Table 1 (1 pt): Increased N₂O flux. Table 1 shows N₂O flux increased from 2.8 to 3.4 kg N₂O-N/ha/yr under conventional tillage (+21.4%). Tillage disrupts soil aggregates, exposing anaerobic microsites where denitrifying bacteria convert soil nitrate to N₂O gas — a potent greenhouse gas (GWP-100 = 265). Conventional tillage also increases nitrogen fertiliser needs (as organic matter cannot supply N), and synthetic fertiliser application elevates soil N₂O emissions further.
Two distinct points needed: mechanism for SOC loss (tillage → aggregate disruption → oxidation → CO₂ release) AND a separate consequence from Table 1 data. N₂O increase is directly readable from the table and ties into GWP/climate impact. Must cite specific data: "from 2.8 to 3.4 kg N₂O-N/ha/yr."
